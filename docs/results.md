# Results and limitations

All numbers come from stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the 10,000 processes of the AI4I 2020
dataset, with the same splits for every approach. The latest numbers for the final model are in
[`reports/metrics.json`][repo], written by `uv run pdm-train`.

## Prediction

| Approach | PR-AUC | F1 | Precision | Recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random guess | 0.034 | – | – | – |
| ML on raw sensor data (gradient boosting) | 0.835 | 0.779 | 80% | 76% |
| ML + physics features (random forest) | 0.891 | 0.877 | 94% | 82% |
| Physics rules only | 0.852 | 0.917 | 100% | 85% |
| Hybrid: rules + ML (M4) | 0.906 | 0.917 | 100% | 85% |
| **Final hybrid: rules + calibrated ML on rows without a rule** | **0.913** | – | – | – |

**Ceiling:** 84.7% of failures are explained by the physics rules. The other 15% (random tool wear
failures and failures without a recorded cause) carry no signal in a single reading. No model can
meaningfully exceed F1 ≈ 0.92 on this data.

## Decisions (cost ratio R = 10)

| Policy | Cost | Savings vs. running to failure |
|---|---|---|
| Running to failure | 3,390 | – |
| ML on raw sensor data | 1,055 | 68.9% |
| Physics rules only | 807 | 76.2% |
| Calibrated hybrid, alarm if p > threshold | 798 | 76.5% |
| **Physics rules + tool change at 225 min** | **763** | **77.5%** |

For R ≥ 10 the rules + tool change policy is always the cheapest, and its lead grows with R: 94%
savings at R = 100, compared with 84% for the rules alone.

## Key findings

1. **Explainability can find physics.** SHAP turned a black box into three precise hypotheses, and
   all of them held exactly.
2. **Domain features beat model complexity.** Adding power, strain and temperature difference helped
   more than any choice of algorithm.
3. **Know your model's blind spots.** Histogram-based gradient boosting cannot represent sharp limits
   at rare extremes, even with the right feature.
4. **Check how labels were made.** 27 labels are decided by floating-point rounding in the data
   generator.
5. **Quantify the ceiling before tuning.** 15% of failures carry no signal; chasing them with
   complex models only adds false alarms.
6. **Optimise the decision, not the metric.** The model with the best PR-AUC is not the cheapest
   policy. A simple age-based tool change beats it.
7. **Train on the population you predict for.** The hybrid's probabilities only became reliable once
   its ML part was trained on the rows it is actually used for.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data.** Three of five failure modes are generated by exact rules. Real machines are
  noisier, and perfect rules will rarely exist.
- **Independent snapshots.** Each process is assessed on its own. There is no machine history, no
  sensor trend and no remaining-useful-life estimate.
- **Static cost evaluation.** Replacing tools at 225 minutes would change the data a machine produces
  after deployment. The cost numbers show direction and size, not an exact forecast.
- **Simplified costs.** One cost for every alarm, and an assumed ratio R.
- **Label noise.** 9 failures have no recorded cause, and 27 heat dissipation labels depend on
  floating-point rounding.

## What carries over to real machines

The specific limits (3.5–9 kW, 11,000 min·Nm, 8.6 K) belong to this dataset. The approach
transfers:

1. **Start with a black box, then explain it.** SHAP interaction plots point to the physical
   quantities that matter.
2. **Build those quantities as features** (power, load × wear, temperature differences) and compare
   them with known machine limits from specifications.
3. **Use rules where physics is certain, and ML for the rest**, trained on exactly the cases the
   rules do not cover.
4. **Calibrate** the ML part, so operators can read its output as a probability.
5. **Decide by cost**, and include simple policies such as age-based replacement as a baseline. They
   are hard to beat for failures that happen at random times.
